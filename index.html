CAPSTONE — AEROSPACE / ELECTRICAL

Solar Wing: a solar-augmented UAV for crop monitoring

PROBLEM

Agricultural crop monitoring needs long flight endurance, and a battery-only fixed-wing UAV runs out of air time fast. The capstone team set out to extend endurance by integrating solar cells directly into the wing, without redesigning the aircraft from a blank sheet.

APPROACH

I structured the electrical and aerodynamic sizing as a four-step cascade — Electrical & Propulsion Foundation, Solar Sizing, Wing Integration, Fuselage Synthesis — rather than sizing everything simultaneously, so each decision could be checked before the next depended on it.

The motor was selected before final aircraft weight was known, using class-based thrust-to-weight benchmarks of 0.4:1–0.7:1 from market research rather than waiting on a number that wasn't available yet. Battery configuration (4S vs. 6S) was a genuine trade-off between electrical viability range and minimizing the number of solar cells needed. I sized the array from voltage rather than power — power alone doesn't capture series vs. parallel wiring — landing on a 24-cell minimum using a conservative, measured 0.7 V/cell against the pack's max voltage of 16.8 V. That gave a solar output of roughly 6 A against a ~2.17 A discharge requirement, about 3× margin. Cruise velocity of 15 m/s was taken as a class benchmark rather than derived from scratch, and the SD7037 airfoil was selected with polar data bracketed at 5° and 10° angle of attack.

RESULTS

Flight testing confirmed stable flight, a controlled landing, and solar charging at the expected rate. Telemetry logging fell short of what we wanted, so rather than filling that gap with invented numbers, I reported the results as qualitative confirmation and flagged the missing telemetry as a process failure to fix next time — a decision I stand behind under technical questioning.

HONORS THESIS — HAEG, CFD / TURBOMACHINERY

Turning a $40 pump into a pico-hydro turbine

PROBLEM

A UV-C LED water disinfection system needs power, and running a dedicated turbine down to pico scale is expensive. The question behind my honors thesis: could an off-the-shelf Iwaki MD-10 centrifugal pump, run backwards as a turbine with a modified impeller, reach the ≥30% hydraulic efficiency needed to make that work?

APPROACH

I ran the CFD in ANSYS CFX with TurboGrid meshing, using SST k-ω turbulence and a frozen-rotor interface treatment. The model was anchored against 16 turbine-mode design points pulled from a collaborator's experimental Q–RPM data across two load cases, plus 12 pump-mode points from the manufacturer's own performance curves — so the CFD had real data to validate against in both directions, not just one.

Partway through, I corrected an assumption that had been carried from a prior design review — the impeller has 4 blades, not 6 — which meant re-running geometry and mesh work on the corrected model. From there I compared three impeller variants: the original open impeller, a modified impeller with forward-curved blades, and a semi-open variant.

RESULTS

The original open impeller predicted about 26% hydraulic efficiency at its best turbine-mode point. The forward-curved modification pushed that to roughly 33%, clearing the 30% target the disinfection system needed. The semi-open variant underperformed both, at around 21% — a useful negative result that ruled out that geometry direction.

SEMESTER PROJECT — STRUCTURAL FEA

Verifying the Grand Canyon Skywalk, in ANSYS

PROBLEM

A cantilevered, glass-bottomed observation deck extending 70 feet over the Grand Canyon is exactly the kind of structure where an FEA course wants you to prove you understand what you're claiming, not just get a number out of a solver. The project: model the Skywalk's structure and verify it holds up under service, wind, and thermal loading.

APPROACH

I built the cantilevering steel structure in SolidWorks — hollow box beams, square cross-members, a swept horseshoe tip — and the glass deck panel as a separate body, then brought the assembly into ANSYS Mechanical with SHELL181 elements throughout. Steel was modeled as A572 Grade 50, glass as tempered laminate.

The interesting decision was the glass-to-steel interface: an early bonded-contact model produced unrealistic stress islands at the connection. Switching to frictional contact (μ = 0.15) to represent the actual neoprene gasket resolved that and gave results I could defend. I then ran gravity plus live load (100 lb/ft²), a wind load case (0.178 psi), and four thermal cases from −7°C to 49°C — each rerun with gravity and live load included, since thermal effects don't act in isolation on a real structure.

RESULTS

Baseline tip deflection came out to about 1.8 in, ranging from 1.68 in at the hottest thermal case to 2.04 in at the coldest — thermal expansion measurably stiffens or relaxes the deflection. Peak von Mises stress was about 10,000 psi against a 345 MPa yield, and glass stress reached about 2,500 psi against a 17,400 psi tensile limit — both well inside margin. Wind load turned out to be a non-factor. A hand calculation predicting roughly 1.17 in of gravity-only deflection served as a sanity check against the full FEA model; I treated the gap between the two as an expected, explainable difference in scope rather than an error to chase down, and excluded the stress singularities at the fixed boundary from any engineering conclusion, per St. Venant's principle.

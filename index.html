UNDERGRADUATE HONORS THESIS — HAEG, CFD / TURBOMACHINERY

Turning a pump into a pico-hydro turbine

PROBLEM

A UV-C LED water disinfection system needs power, and running a dedicated turbine down to pico scale is expensive. My honors thesis asked whether an off-the-shelf Iwaki MD-10 centrifugal pump, run backwards as a turbine with a modified impeller, could reach the ≥30% hydraulic efficiency needed to drive one.

APPROACH

I ran the CFD in ANSYS CFX with TurboGrid meshing, SST k-ω turbulence, and a frozen-rotor interface. Pump-mode simulations covered three flow rates (5.7–10.2 LPM) across four speeds (1,000–3,500 RPM) and matched the manufacturer's published curves. Turbine-mode simulations used 16 design points pulled from a labmate's experimental data across two load cases (100 Ω / 200 Ω).

The pump's original backward-curved impeller peaked at about 55% efficiency in pump mode — but only 26% in turbine mode, a 29-point drop caused by flow separation and recirculation where the blade geometry meets the reversed flow. Mirroring the blade curvature from backward- to forward-curved in SolidWorks, without changing anything else, and re-running the same 16 points, cut that recirculation down and shifted the best-efficiency point to a higher flow rate — consistent with what's reported in the PAT literature.

RESULTS

The forward-curved impeller reached about 33% peak hydraulic efficiency at 16 LPM / 3,400 RPM — a 7-point gain that clears the 30% threshold above roughly 14–15 LPM for both load cases. That's a CFD result, not a validated one: it excludes mechanical and generator losses, and the modified impeller hasn't been built and tested yet. Fabricating and experimentally testing it is the direct next step, running in parallel with a labmate's experimental rig.

NASA LANGLEY RESEARCH CENTER — DEPLOYABLE STRUCTURES FOR SPACE APPLICATIONS

Three deployable-structures projects, one summer

I spent the internship across three related projects under PI Dr. Juan M. Fernandez, alongside fellow interns Julianne Miana and Maxim Somov — all variations on the same underlying problem: how do you pack a large structure small enough to launch, then get it to reliably unfold once it's there?

APRM — robotically assembled antenna reflector

Worked on the composite booms — U-shaped slit-booms made with shape-memory epoxy — that connect a reflector's main and side panels as they deploy from a stacked launch configuration into a flower-like final shape.

Ground testing used a helium-balloon 0g offloading rig, calibrated per panel to cancel gravity, plus a lubricated table and a preloaded spring latch to secure each panel at full extension.

DOPPLER — lunar communications tower

Helped test the base leveler and the deployment/stabilization system for a tall deployable tower meant to give lunar instruments line-of-sight over uneven terrain, using a Vicon motion-capture rig to track the payload platform as the boom extended.

Testing surfaced real limitations: the leveler needed more lift and torque than it had, and the boom had a maximum stable height the adaptive controller needed tuning around.

Solar Sail — damage characterization & folding

Solar sails are reflective, chromium/aluminum-coated PEN sheets only 2–2.5 microns thick — thin enough that handling alone damages them. I mapped micro- and macro-tears across a 20 m² sail quadrant and worked through repair and folding procedures.

Repairs used aluminum-covered PEN patches with heat-set adhesive and Teflon sheet; each sail folded down to fit on a pair of 10-inch spools, a real constraint on how large a sail can be ground-tested.

TEAM PROJECT — STRUCTURAL FEA, WITH CALEB BENCH, KAN SUZUKI & SAHIL SURA

Verifying the Grand Canyon Skywalk, in ANSYS

PROBLEM

The Grand Canyon Skywalk is a horseshoe-shaped, glass-bottomed bridge cantilevered 70 ft past the canyon rim with nothing underneath it, 4,000 ft above the canyon floor. It has to withstand 100 mph winds from any direction, an M8.0 earthquake, and crowds of up to 120 people — exactly the kind of structure where you want more than a hand calculation before trusting a claim. Our team's project: evaluate its structural performance under realistic loading using FEA.

APPROACH

We built the cantilevering steel structure in SolidWorks — hollow box beams (32×72 in, 2 in walls), cross-members, a swept semicircular tip — and modeled the 2.79 in, 5-layer laminated glass deck as a solid plate, then brought the assembly into ANSYS Mechanical with SHELL181 elements throughout. Steel was A572 Grade 50; the fixed boundary condition replaced the real foundation of 96 rock anchors driven 46 ft into limestone.

The interesting decision was the glass-to-steel interface: an early bonded-contact model produced unrealistic stress islands at the connection. Switching to frictional contact (μ = 0.15) to represent the actual neoprene gasket resolved that. From there we ran gravity + crowd live load (100 lb/ft²), a 100 mph wind case (0.178 psi), four thermal cases spanning Arizona's seasonal range (−7°C to 49°C), and a modal analysis for the first six natural frequencies.

RESULTS

Baseline tip deflection came out to 1.81 in (≈L/468) — within 12% of a gravity-only hand calculation used as a sanity check. Peak Von Mises stress was 9,172 psi against a 50,000 psi yield, a factor of safety of about 5.4. Wind changed deformation by only 0.03% — this is a gravity-dominated structure, not a wind-dominated one. The thermal cases were the most interesting result: at −7°C, restrained contraction pushed tip deflection up to 2.04 in, while at 49°C, restrained expansion actually cambered the tip upward, dropping deflection to 1.68 in — a real thermal-camber effect documented in long-span cantilevers. Modal analysis found natural frequencies from 3.26 Hz to 13.55 Hz across the first six modes, evolving from global bending to torsional and local "drumming" modes, all at low enough amplitude to indicate a stiff, stable structure. We excluded the stress singularity at the fixed boundary from all conclusions, per St. Venant's principle.

TEAM PROJECT — OHIO STATE FIRST-YEAR ENGINEERING, WITH JON, NEIL & RILEY

Solar Wing: a solar-augmented UAV for crop scouting

PROBLEM

Farmers monitoring crops by drone run into the same wall every time — battery life. Our four-person team's brief: design, build, and flight-test a proof-of-concept fixed-wing UAV that uses wing-integrated solar cells to meaningfully extend flight time, with in-flight charging and live video for crop scouting.

APPROACH

We structured the electrical and aerodynamic sizing as what we called the Engineering Thought Plan — Electrical Foundation, Propulsion Optimization, Solar Sizing, Wing Integration, then Fuselage Synthesis — so each decision could be checked before the next one depended on it, landing on an SD7037 airfoil and a 24-cell minimum solar array sized conservatively from measured cell voltage rather than nameplate power.

My part centered on the solar/power testing and the post-flight data analysis. I ran the in-flight solar charging comparison and, after the test flight, dug into why the airframe didn't hold up the way we expected.

RESULTS

Solar charging worked better than expected: at 50% throttle, ten minutes of flight dropped the battery only 0.23 V with solar assist, versus 1.39 V without it — roughly a sixfold reduction, and clean confirmation the array was pulling real weight. Live video transmission held throughout. The test flight itself didn't go as planned — the wing's structural support hadn't accounted for how the drone would be transported and reassembled at the flight site, and a back-spar failure kept us from sustained flight. Tracing that back afterward, the root cause was a transportability gap in the design, not the solar or electrical system — the kind of finding you don't catch until you've actually moved real hardware to a real field.
